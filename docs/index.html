Screen 05 — case studies

Two days that changed how Sri Lanka schedules power

Written for readers who have never thought about a power grid. No jargon that is not explained on the spot.

CASE 01 9 FEBRUARY 2025

The Sunday the whole country went dark

Electricity cannot be stored at scale. Whatever the country uses at 11 in the morning has to be made at 11 in the morning, to the megawatt. That balance is held by very heavy machines spinning in step with each other — turbines the size of a bus, turning fifty times a second.

Their weight is the point. When something goes wrong, all that spinning mass keeps turning for a moment and buys the grid a few seconds to react. Engineers call this inertia. Solar panels have none of it. They are silent, and they stop instantly.

A Sunday in February is the worst combination available: factories closed, so demand is low, and a clear sky, so solar output is high. With so much of the load met by panels, few heavy machines were needed, so few were running. When a fault hit, there was not enough spinning mass to ride through it. The grid came apart, and the country lost power for most of the day.

CASE 02 2025 – 2026

Switching off free electricity, on purpose

Since the blackout, the utility has kept more heavy machines running than demand strictly requires. Those machines have a floor: a coal unit cannot be turned down below roughly a third of its output without shutting off altogether, and shutting off means hours before it can come back.

So on a bright Sunday, the sums stop working. The floor beneath the running machines, plus everything the sun is producing, adds up to more electricity than the country wants. Something has to give, and the thing that gives is solar — farms are told to disconnect. Operators put their losses at around Rs 2 billion.

It is not waste through carelessness. It is the cost of a stability rule meeting an inflexible fleet. But it is a cost, it is growing, and it is worth putting a number on.

Try it yourself

On the Rule Sandbox, raise the minimum synchronous units and watch the yellow curtailment line appear over midday. That line is solar being switched off.

What this tool finds

The curtailment is a scheduling outcome, not a solar problem. On our study day the same stability requirement can be met with a different set of machines online — one that leaves room for the sun. The gap between those two answers is the part worth arguing about.

The Rs 2 bn figure is as reported by solar operators in public statements; it is not produced by this tool.

Known limitations — tell us what else is missing
  • No transmission network. A substitution the tool proposes may be impossible on the wires.
  • No hydro reservoir dynamics across days. Water is budgeted within the day only.
  • Mini hydro, wind and rooftop solar are fleet aggregates, not individual units.
  • Rooftop solar output is an estimate published by CEB, not a measurement.
  • Ramp rates and start-up trajectories are not modelled.
  • Fuel prices are flat across the day and do not reflect contracts or take-or-pay terms.
Every one of these is a reason a figure here could be wrong. If your objection is not on this list, it belongs on it.
